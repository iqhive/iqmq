    nsq¶

    Maturity: Experimental · Tier: 3 · Owner: @qoke

    Client & version policy¶

    The native baselines for this driver live in benchmarks/native-baselines/ and measure the clients below against the harness's nsq image (make bench-native CLIENTS="nsq"). A driver number is only comparable with a baseline taken from the same client version, broker version and confirmation boundary, so the pins move together; each baseline's package comment records the settlement boundary it measures as well.

    Baseline Client Pinned version Confirmation boundary
    nsq github.com/nsqio/go-nsq github.com/nsqio/go-nsq@v1.1.0 nsqd OK response to PUB/MPUB (memory-backed until mem-queue-size)

    Verified facts (the API contract)¶

    Checked while building the native baselines; re-verify when a pin moves.

    • size limits. nsqd's defaults cap a single message at 1 MiB (--max-msg-size) and a whole command body at 5 MiB (--max-body-size), so an MPUB of 100 64 KiB messages (6.4 MiB) must be split into smaller chunks. Verified against: nsqd v1.3.0 flag defaults; verified by the MPUB chunking in the nsq baseline.
    • deferred publish and discovery. go-nsq exposes per-message delays through Producer.DeferredPublish (DPUB) and discovers nsqd instances through nsqlookupd, which requires nsqd to broadcast an address the client can reach. Verified against: go-nsq v1.1.0 producer.go:233; conformance/harness/brokers.go NSQ.
    • output buffering. nsqd buffers writes to each client and flushes them on a timeout the client sets in IDENTIFY: go-nsq defaults to output_buffer_size 16 KiB and output_buffer_timeout 250 ms, so a single small message can take a quarter of a second to arrive on an otherwise idle connection while a 64 KiB message fills the buffer and leaves at once. A latency-sensitive consumer must lower the timeout, and the client warns that going below 25 ms costs nsqd noticeable CPU. Verified against: go-nsq v1.1.0 config.go:165-172; visible in BenchmarkNative/nsq/EndToEnd, where 64 B and 1 KiB round trips take ~250 ms and 64 KiB takes ~380 us.
    • channels. A topic with no channel discards what is published to it, so a channel must exist (SUB, or the /channel/create HTTP endpoint) before the first publish or messages are lost. Verified against: nsqd v1.3.0 behaviour; verified by the nsq baseline's provisioning step.

    Topic / subscription mapping¶

    iqmq NSQ
    topic an nsqd topic, Config.TopicPrefix prepended verbatim
    subscription an nsqd channel
    Message.Key nothing native — see Headers & limits
    Message.Headers nothing native — see Headers & limits
    DeliveryInfo.Attempt nsqd's Attempts, 1 on the first delivery
    DeliveryInfo.LeaseExpires the receipt time plus the negotiated msg_timeout
    DeliveryInfo.Partition, Offset, Sequence never set: nsqd has none of them

    A channel is both the fan-out and the consumer group: every channel of a topic receives a copy of every message, and every consumer of one channel competes for its messages. A subscription therefore cannot be empty — there is no such thing as an unnamed channel — which is the opposite of the SQS profile, where the subscription is empty because a queue has no fan-out.

    The mapping is a pure prepend and never an escape. nsqd's grammar is ^[.a-zA-Z0-9_-]+(#ephemeral)?$, one to sixty-four bytes, and a portable topic outside it is refused rather than transformed: a mapper that replaced the illegal bytes would map a/b and a-b onto one NSQ topic, and a caller would then find one subscription receiving another's messages. nsq.CheckTopic is checked against go-nsq's own validator by FuzzNSQTopicName, because this driver validates by hand — one regular-expression match per publication is not free — and two spellings of one grammar drift the day somebody edits either.

    A topic carrying a pattern metacharacter (*, ?, #, >) is refused with ErrUnsupported and not with "invalid topic": NSQ has no pattern subscription at all, and the feature being missing is the more useful thing to tell a caller who wrote a pattern on purpose. A trailing #ephemeral is not a pattern.

    Topology¶

    nsqd creates a topic on the first publication to it whatever any client asks, which is a property of the broker rather than a choice this driver makes. The channel is not created that way, and that matters: a topic holds its messages until it has at least one channel and then hands the backlog to the channel that appears — a second channel created afterwards starts empty. So a consumer that subscribes after the publications it wants has missed them.

    Config.EnsureTopology is the deliberate creation, through nsqd's HTTP API, and it is off by default. The specification's "no hidden topology creation" is about a driver that could provision and chooses not to; the conformance harness turns it on, because there the kit is the operator.

    Publish path & confirmation boundary¶

    Publish is PUB, PublishBatch is MPUB, and a publication carrying a delay is DPUB. All three are answered by nsqd with OK, and this driver waits for that answer.

    The boundary is: nsqd has taken the message into the topic it names. That queue is in memory up to nsqd's --mem-queue-size and on disk beyond it, so OK survives a consumer crash and does not survive an nsqd crash with an in-memory queue.

    mode Honoured as
    ConfirmNone the same round trip: go-nsq has no publish this driver could use without giving up the answer
    ConfirmDefault, ConfirmAccepted nsqd's OK
    ConfirmDurable refused when the sink is opened, with ErrUnsupported

    A caller's context bounds the wait and not the work: the frame is written by go-nsq's asynchronous form and the driver selects on the answer and on the context. A context that expires after the frame is on the wire makes the outcome ambiguous — IsAmbiguous is true and Retryable is false — because nsqd may well have taken the publication, and reporting "did not publish" would make a duplicate look like a loss.

    The context does not bound a connection go-nsq has to make first. go-nsq dials and sends IDENTIFY inside the publish call, and neither takes a context, so the first publication on an endpoint, and the first after its connection dropped, waits for the dial and for nsqd's answer to IDENTIFY however soon its context ends. For a plain handshake that is up to Config.DialTimeout plus Config.ReadTimeout, 65 seconds with the defaults. A TLS upgrade, Snappy or Deflate, and AUTH each add reads of their own, and each of those is bounded by Config.ReadTimeout again. Opening a consumer makes the same connections and has the same wait.

    A connection that fails there, through an EOF, a reset or a timeout, is CodeUnavailable and retryable, because the publication was never written; only nsqd refusing what the handshake declared, or a TLS certificate refused on either side, is CodeInvalidConfig. An address that is not nsqd's TCP port at all, nsqd's HTTP port for one, fails the same way and so also reads as a retryable outage: nothing in the error tells it from an nsqd that is restarting. It is also costly to retry. go-nsq allocates whatever frame size the first four bytes of the answer claim before it reads the frame, and an HTTP answer's HTTP claims about 1.13 GiB, so every attempt against such an address allocates that much. go-nsq makes the connection itself, and the driver has no point at which to look at the answer first.

    There is no AsyncSink and no FlushSink: a publication is a completed round trip by the time Publish returns, so there is nothing admitted to flush.

    With several nsqd addresses configured, publications go round-robin and there is no failover: the specification gives retry policy to the core, and a driver that tried the next endpoint would turn one publication into two attempts with the core's own count blind to the second. The core's next attempt lands on the next endpoint anyway, because the cursor has moved.

    Batch splitting¶

    MPUB is one command carrying many bodies and one answer, so a partial outcome is not something nsqd reports. A batch larger than Config.MaxBodyBytes (nsqd's --max-body-size) is split into as many MPUBs as it needs, counting the command's own framing, and each piece is its own command with its own outcome. A batch can therefore half succeed, and the *BatchError's Errs are index-aligned with the messages — every index a failed command carried gets that command's error, and no index gets another message's outcome.

    A piece of one message is sent as a PUB. MPUB adds eight bytes of framing to a lone message, so with --max-body-size equal to --max-msg-size a message at the advertised MaxMessageBytes would be over the body limit, and nsqd answers that with a fatal E_BAD_BODY that also closes the connection under every other publication on it.

    A batch carrying a delay cannot use MPUB at all, because DPUB takes one body; such a batch is published one message at a time.

    FeatureAtomicBatchPublish is not claimed.

    Consume path¶

    nsq.Consumer.AddConcurrentHandlers delivers by calling the driver and the SPI asks the driver to be called, so driverkit.PushAdapter sits between them:

    • MaxInFlight is the core's resolved prefetch, so nsqd's own RDY accounting is the primary bound and the ring is sized to hold everything it can send;
    • the ring's capacity is that prefetch, and a push waits when it is full of live deliveries, which is the backpressure a broker that delivers by callback understands. A push that finds the ring full first evicts the deliveries that have gone dead in it, the ones Fetch would drop (below). Each of those is a message nsqd no longer counts against this consumer, so nsqd has already sent the delivery that needs its place, and a push that waited behind it would leave go-nsq holding the next one with nsqd's timeout running and the driver's lease not started. A push that evicts more than it needs wakes the pushes already waiting, and so do a Fetch and a give-back by the lease sweeper;
    • the handler pool is min(prefetch, 8). It does not need to be the prefetch: a handler goroutine only moves a delivery into the ring, and what the pool size bounds is how many deliveries sit in a blocked push — each of them a message whose nsqd timeout is running down while nothing works on it, so fewer is safer rather than slower.

    DisableAutoResponse is set on every delivery, before anything else can return, and the handler always returns nil. Both halves are load-bearing: without the first, go-nsq's handler loop would FIN the message the moment the callback returned — before the application had seen it — and with an error return it would requeue the message with a backoff of its own.

    A delivery whose body this driver cannot decode is requeued with a delay and counted, because nsqd's verbs are finish, requeue and touch and none of them says "this is not my message". Backend.Undecodable() is the count. The health state does not change: the connection that carried the record is healthy, and a degraded state would make the next successful Ping report a reconnection.

    Fetch drops a delivery that went dead while it sat in the ring — one whose lease expired, or which a later delivery of the same message superseded. Handing it out would make the handler's work certainly wasted and its acknowledgement certainly refused. Backend.Stale() counts them.

    Discovery¶

    Config.LookupdAddrs is preferred over Config.Addrs whenever it is set, and the preference is not a tie-break: a consumer connected straight to the nsqd addresses never learns about an nsqd that joins the cluster later, so on a cluster a direct connection silently consumes a subset of the topic. With one nsqd the two are equivalent and the direct form is one fewer moving part. Publication always goes to Config.Addrs: nsqlookupd is a discovery service for consumers and cannot be published through.

    Ack / nack / extend mapping¶

    iqmq NSQ Notes
    Ack FIN
    Nack (requeue) REQ(delay) nsqd's own delayed redelivery
    Nack (drop) FIN the specification calls it destructive and it is: finishing is nsqd's only way to make a message go away
    Nack (dead letter) — ErrUnsupported; see Dead-letter queue
    Extend TOUCH not a settlement
    batch settlement — nsqd has no batched verb, so no BatchSettler

    nsqd answers none of the three. It replies to PUB, MPUB and DPUB and to nothing else, so the settlement boundary here is the frame was handed to the connection. Every correctness question a broker would otherwise answer is therefore answered locally, before the frame is written, and this is the part of the driver worth reading twice.

    The local lease check¶

    nsqd will accept a FIN for a message whose timeout has already expired, and after a redelivery that FIN finishes another consumer's work. There is no verb that asks "do I still hold this?". The specification makes "lets a stale ack settle a later redelivery" a disqualifier rather than a rough edge, so the driver keeps:

    • a local view of each delivery's deadline, moved by TOUCH and read before every settlement, with Config.LeaseGuard of slack;
    • a map from nsqd and message identifier to the delivery it currently vouches for, so a redelivery supersedes its predecessor the moment it arrives. The nsqd is part of the key because an identifier is unique only within the nsqd that issued it: two nsqds with the same node ID can issue the same one;
    • a delivery generation, so a settlement carrying a stale one is refused.

    A settlement for a delivery that fails any of these is refused with ErrLeaseLost and nothing is written at that moment. A delivery that failed only the deadline is given back with REQ(0) shortly afterwards, for the reason under The lease sweeper. A second settlement of a delivery that is still ours is ErrAlreadySettled, which is a different answer for a different reason: one says the work was accounted for once, the other says it was never accounted for and the message is coming back.

    The lease sweeper¶

    The driver gives a delivery back one LeaseGuard before its deadline, with REQ(0), if nothing has settled or extended it. It costs nothing that expiry would not have cost — nsqd would have requeued the message a moment later — and it turns the local check from a diagnosis into a prevention: with it, a delivery is superseded only by a redelivery the driver did not see coming.

    Config.LeaseGuard is therefore two things at once, and a caller using the core's lease extension has to keep them in step: it is how much slack the settlement check demands, and it is how early the delivery is given back. A caller that opts into mq.WithLeaseExtension(lead) must choose a lead LONGER than LeaseGuard, or the driver requeues the delivery before the core gets round to extending it and a long handler never keeps its lease at all. The default lead is zero — extension is opt-in — so the default configuration has nothing to trip over.

    The guard also bounds the deadline. A consumer's own AckDeadline has to be longer than LeaseGuard, as the backend's does: at or below it, every delivery is inside its guard the moment it arrives, so Fetch drops it and the sweeper gives it back, and nothing is ever handed out. Opening such a consumer is refused with CodeInvalidConfig. MinAckDeadline is below the default LeaseGuard, so the shortest deadline nsqd accepts is one of them unless the guard is lowered too.

    Once a deadline has passed, nsqd may already have redelivered the message, so the driver never writes a FIN or TOUCH for that delivery. It still owes nsqd an answer, and that matters more than it sounds: go-nsq counts one response per delivery on each connection, does not finish closing a connection until that count is zero, and does not reconnect to that nsqd until the close has finished. An unanswered delivery on a connection that drops therefore stops the subscription receiving from that nsqd at all. go-nsq does not tell the driver when a connection is closing, so the driver cannot wait for the moment a frame is harmless.

    When a redelivery arrives and supersedes the old delivery, the old one's debt is chained onto the delivery that superseded it and paid once that one has been answered, when nsqd no longer holds the identifier in flight and the frame is refused harmlessly. When none arrives, the lease sweeper answers the old delivery with REQ(0) on its next pass but one, one to two sweep intervals (half a LeaseGuard each) after a settlement or a Fetch found the lease gone. The wait gives a redelivery that is already on its way time to arrive and take the debt over, which is the common case shortly after a deadline.

    nsqd accepts a REQ only from the client holding the message, so the sweeper's frame never touches another consumer's delivery, and on a dropped connection it goes nowhere but still settles go-nsq's count, so the consumer reconnects. The one case where it changes anything is a redelivery to this same connection that nsqd sent and the driver has still not received when the sweep comes round. nsqd requeues that redelivery, the driver hands it out as live, and its Ack later finds nothing in flight at nsqd, so the message is delivered once more. That is a duplicate and never a loss, and it is why the frame is a REQ: a FIN in the same place would finish a message whether or not anyone had done its work.

    TOUCH is not an addition¶

    nsqd's TOUCH resets a message's timeout to the channel's negotiated msg_timeout, measured from now, and caps a message's total in-flight time at --max-msg-timeout from its first delivery. So:

    • what a granted Extend(by) buys is the channel's timeout from now, whatever by asked for, so a by longer than that timeout is refused with CodeInvalidArgument and nothing is written. The SPI promises at least by, and nsqd would redeliver the message one timeout later underneath a handler that was told it had longer. The timeout is the consumer's AckDeadline, which is the msg_timeout its connection negotiates, and it is exactly DeliveryInfo.LeaseExpires minus ReceivedAt. That difference is what the core's lease scheduler asks for, so its extensions are never refused;
    • Extend(by) is also refused when it would end past Config.MaxAckDeadline from the delivery, which mirrors that cap. Capabilities().Limit(LimitMaxLease) is that cap, the longest a delivery can be held across many extensions, and not what one extension buys;
    • Extend(0) or less is CodeInvalidArgument: nsqd would reset the timeout to the channel's default, which for a short remaining lease is an extension nobody asked for and for a long one is a shortening.

    An extension leaves the delivery settleable. The settler has a peek beside its claim for exactly that: a driver whose Extend took the delivery's state would make the acknowledgement that follows it fail, and the message would be processed twice for no reason but the extension meant to prevent it.

    Ordering¶

    No ordering is claimed, in any scope. the specification's "ordering never claimed" is literal here: nsqd makes no promise about the order in which a channel's messages are delivered, not even for one producer, one consumer and no failures — an in-memory queue and a disk-backed queue are drained independently, and a requeue puts a message back wherever there is room.

    A Conditional claim with a note would be the wrong answer, because there is no condition under which it holds. A caller that needs ordering needs the core's hash lanes over a broker that has it, or another broker.

    Delays¶

    Native, in both directions:

    • a publication delay is DPUB, bounded by Config.MaxRequeueDelay (nsqd's --max-req-timeout, default one hour) and advertised as the specification's MaxDelay. PublishOptions.DeliverAt is converted to a delay against the injected clock, and an instant already past is "deliver now" rather than an error;
    • a redelivery delay is REQ(delay), bounded the same way.

    RequeueWithoutBackoff and not Requeue: go-nsq's Requeue sets the consumer's RDY to zero for the whole subscription while a backoff of its own runs, so one message's failure would stop every other message. The delay is the core's and the client library does not get a second one.

    Scheduled publication is nsqd's own and needs no emulation, so nothing here is EmulatedVolatile and no Emulation policy is consulted.

    Dead-letter queue¶

    No native route. FeatureDeadLetter means the driver knows where a dead letter goes and can get it there in one native settlement, and nsqd has no verb for it — so Nack(NackDeadLetter()) is refused with ErrUnsupported rather than emulated inside the driver.

    the specification's "ED" for this broker is the core's emulation: mq.WithDeadLetter publishes the message to a topic and then acknowledges the original, which classes as semantics-preserving and which needs nothing from this driver. It carries the duplicate risk the specification requires to be disclosed: the publication and the acknowledgement are two operations and a failure between them means the message exists in both places.

    mq.WithNativeDeadLetter() is refused by the core against this driver, which is the rule that a capability the driver does not have is refused rather than emulated in silence.

    Headers & limits¶

    NSQ has no headers at all — not a limited set, not string-only values. The protocol carries a body and nothing else. So the promise of ordered, duplicate-preserving, binary-capable headers is kept here by the portable envelope of github.com/iqhive/iqmq/wire and by nothing else, and the same goes for the message key, the message identifier and the typed properties.

    the four modes therefore reduce to three behaviours:

    Mode Behaviour
    MetadataNativeStrict (default) a message carrying any header, identifier, typed property or key — including an empty key, because the specification makes nil and empty different — is refused with ErrUnsupported before any I/O, naming the field
    MetadataPortableEnvelope every message is enveloped; a non-iqmq consumer reads the envelope rather than the body
    MetadataAdaptive the messages that carry metadata are enveloped and a bare body is left alone
    MetadataLenientDrop the metadata is discarded and the body is published; Backend.DroppedMetadata() counts what went

    Config.Metadata is the deployment's one-time "yes, envelope on this broker". The specification makes the header-less envelope an interop-affecting emulation, available under EmulationAll or an explicit metadata mode, and the SPI carries no emulation policy at all — so there is nowhere else to say it once rather than on every publisher and consumer. A handle that names a mode keeps it: the field replaces the zero value and nothing else, so an explicit MetadataPortableEnvelope is never downgraded and an explicit MetadataLenientDrop is never upgraded. A handle that names MetadataNativeStrict explicitly is indistinguishable from one that named nothing, which is why the conformance run documents metadata/native-strict-rejection as inapplicable.

    With the envelope configured, the three header features are advertised as EmulatedDurable with the emulation stated, because the headers really do travel and are as durable as the message. Without it they are Unsupported.

    Two of this driver's reasons to envelope are invisible to driverkit.Envelope.Needed, which reads the capability table :

    • a message key. The inventory has no native-key feature, so a keyed message with no other metadata answers "nothing to do" and would go out with its key discarded, silently.
    • an empty body. nsqd answers a zero-length PUB with E_BAD_MESSAGE, so the nil-versus-empty distinction survives here only inside an envelope, which is never empty. In the two non-enveloping modes an empty message cannot be published at all, and that is refused before the round trip.

    Under MetadataAdaptive a body that begins with the envelope's magic is enveloped too, even though nsqd could carry it natively. With no headers there is nowhere to record which records are enveloped, so the body is the only evidence there is — and a driver that published such a body natively would decode a payload as metadata on the way back, or leave a record it could neither deliver nor settle. wire.Detector.DetectFull is the read side of the same rule: it claims a body only when the body is, exactly and entirely, a canonical envelope. A producer that deliberately publishes a valid envelope as a payload is still indistinguishable from one that enveloped it; that is a property of a broker with nothing out of band, and drivers/sqs avoids it with a message attribute because it has one.

    Limits¶

    Every limit this driver advertises mirrors an nsqd flag rather than a value nsqd reports: nsqd publishes none of its limits over either the TCP protocol or its HTTP API, so an operator who moves a flag must move the configuration with it.

    limit Config field nsqd flag Default
    MaxMessageBytes MaxMessageBytes --max-msg-size 1 MiB
    MaxBatchBytes MaxBodyBytes --max-body-size 5 MiB
    MaxDelay MaxRequeueDelay --max-req-timeout 1 h
    MaxLease MaxAckDeadline --max-msg-timeout 15 min
    MaxBatchMessages, MaxHeaderCount, MaxHeaderBytes — — not advertised: nsqd bounds no such thing and the specification forbids guessing

    Two more flags are mirrored as refusals rather than as limits, because nsqd enforces them in the IDENTIFY handshake and a client that asks for less has its whole connection refused: Config.OutputBufferTimeout must be at least --min-output-buffer-timeout (25 ms) and an ack deadline at least --min-msg-timeout (1 s). Both are checked by Config.Validate, which is the specification's "validate config before starting background work" — the alternative is every operation on the client failing with an error about a handshake.

    The size check is applied to what will be written, which for an enveloped message is the envelope: a 1 MiB body plus its headers is over nsqd's default even though the body alone is not, and finding that out from nsqd costs a round trip and an E_BAD_MESSAGE.

    Reconnect¶

    go-nsq owns the reconnection, in both halves: a producer redials on the next publication, and a consumer either re-polls nsqlookupd or retries the direct address every Config.LookupdPollInterval. This driver adds no loop of its own, which is why there is no driverkit.Reconnector here.

    A consumer closed while go-nsq is still making one of its connections has to keep that connection from receiving. go-nsq's stop counts only the connections that have finished their handshake, and one that finishes after the stop would ask nsqd for messages and hand the first to a handler pool that has gone, which crashes the process. So Close first sets the consumer's in-flight limit to zero, and a connection that finishes afterwards asks for nothing. A Close that reaches a consumer OpenSource is still connecting leaves the teardown to OpenSource, which closes it once go-nsq has returned and reports ErrClosed. A connection go-nsq started on its own, a redial or one a lookupd poll discovered, is out of the driver's reach, and what it leaves behind depends on what else was connected:

    • with no other connection open when Close stops the consumer, which is the case for one nsqd, the late connection stays open and idle, with go-nsq's read and write goroutines for it, until nsqd or the network closes it. That is one connection and two goroutines for each Close that lands inside such a handshake;
    • with another connection still open, which happens with several nsqds in Config.Addrs or with nsqlookupd, go-nsq waits for the late connection too before it finishes stopping. Close then returns only after Config.APITimeout, and go-nsq's handler pool, min(prefetch, 8) goroutines, stays alive with the idle connection for as long as nsqd keeps it open.

    Neither receives a message, because the in-flight limit is zero. Closing the late connection needs a change to go-nsq itself: its stop has to count connections still in their handshake, and a handshake that finishes after the stop has to close its connection.

    The consequence is stated rather than hidden: go-nsq offers no reconnection callback, so the specification's OnReconnect has only one source on this driver — a Ping that succeeds after a failure. It is coarse, and a caller that never calls Ping never sees one. Backend.Health() is what carries the state, and Backend.Reconnects() and Backend.DegradedTransitions() are the numbers.

    A lost connection does not make nsqd requeue what that connection was holding. Each message stays in flight until its own timeout, AckDeadline after nsqd sent it, and only then goes back to the channel, so another consumer receives it up to an ack deadline after the connection went. A settlement for a delivery from the lost connection is written nowhere: go-nsq discards it with the connection and the driver reports success, because the frame was handed to the connection (see At-least-once window). A delivery whose message has come back is superseded, and a settlement for it is refused with ErrLeaseLost, which is the same machinery as an expired lease and is tested the same way.

    Config.HeartbeatInterval must be less than Config.ReadTimeout or go-nsq reads with a deadline nsqd's heartbeat cannot beat, and every idle connection looks dead. Validate refuses it.

    At-least-once window¶

    The window is between nsqd accepting a message and the driver's FIN reaching it, and a message is delivered again whenever that window is interrupted:

    • the per-message timeout expires — or the lease sweeper hands the message back one guard earlier, deliberately;
    • the connection holding it goes away, in which case nsqd makes each message on it available again when that message's timeout expires;
    • the source is closed, which requeues everything it has handed out and never settled;
    • the lease sweeper gives back a delivery whose lease has passed, and its REQ lands on a redelivery still on its way to the same connection (see The lease sweeper);
    • a REQ for any reason, including the core's retry schedule.

    A duplicate is normal and an ordering change is normal with it. There is no de-duplication and no idempotent publish: FeatureDeduplication, FeatureIdempotentPublish and FeatureExactlyOnceDelivery are all unsupported, and a handler has to be idempotent.

    Two narrower windows are worth naming. A publication whose context expires while the frame is in flight is ambiguous, so a core retry of it may produce a second message. And a FIN is a frame nsqd does not answer, so a FIN lost with the connection produces a redelivery of a message the application had finished.

    A TOUCH lost with the connection is the same kind of loss and costs more. go-nsq discards a TOUCH for a delivery on a connection that is closing and does not tell the driver, so Extend reports success while nsqd keeps the original timeout. When it expires nsqd hands the message to another consumer, and two handlers work on it at once, which is what extending the lease was meant to prevent. The unanswered delivery also keeps go-nsq from finishing the close, so the subscription does not reconnect to that nsqd while the handler goes on extending.

    Config & DSN¶

    client, err := nsq.Open(ctx, nsq.Config{
    Addrs:        []string{"nsqd-a:4150", "nsqd-b:4150"},
    LookupdAddrs: []string{"http://lookupd-a:4161", "http://lookupd-b:4161"},
    AdminAddrs:   []string{"nsqd-a:4151", "nsqd-b:4151"},
    Metadata:     mq.MetadataAdaptive,
    AckDeadline:  30 * time.Second,
})

    The DSN form is the same configuration as a connection string (nsq.ParseDSN). The authority is the nsqd TCP endpoints, a host with no port takes 4150, and the path is Config.TopicPrefix:

    nsq://nsqd-a:4150,nsqd-b:4150/acme.?lookupd=http://lookupd:4161&metadata=adaptive&ackdeadline=30s

    The parameters are listed on ParseDSN, and an unknown one is a construction error naming it. Three decisions in the DSN are not obvious:

    • lookupd= and admin= are comma-separated lists, because the shared parser refuses a repeated key outright and that refusal is right for every scalar it was written for ;
    • max_in_flight is not a parameter, though the example DSN shows one. Prefetch is the core's: it is resolved from the consumer configuration and handed to the driver, and a DSN that set it would be a second owner of one number that the driver would then have to choose between, silently ;
    • Config.AdminAddrs, when empty, is derived from the TCP addresses by adding one to each port — nsqd's own default layout, and a guess rather than a discovery, which is why the field is explicit.

    Config.AuthSecret is a dsn.Secret: neither Config.String(), nor Config.LogValue(), nor Config.DSN(), nor any error this driver builds can carry it.

    Administration¶

    Client.Admin() is nsqd's HTTP API — /topic/create, /topic/delete, /channel/create, /channel/delete, /channel/empty, /stats — because that is where nsqd puts topology; the TCP protocol has none.

    Every one of those endpoints is answered by one nsqd about itself. nsqlookupd knows which nsqd holds a topic but cannot create, delete or purge anything, so an administrative call has to reach every nsqd the deployment has: this driver sends each one to every address in Config.AdminAddrs and reports the first failure, and Stats sums what they answer. A deployment whose AdminAddrs name a subset of its nsqd instances gets a partial answer.

    QueueStats.Lag is never known: a channel holds what has not been finished and keeps nothing else, so there is no position in a log to be behind. The specification makes that an unknown rather than a zero.

    The HTTP requests go over a transport of the backend's own, cloned from http.DefaultTransport when the backend opens: a proxy or dialer an application set there still applies, nothing else in the process can close its connections under a request in flight, and Close releases them. An admin call after Close returns ErrClosed. With Config.TLS set the requests are HTTPS and verify each nsqd with Config.TLS against that nsqd's own host name, exactly as the protocol connection does -- a ServerName in Config.TLS is ignored by both.

    nsqlookupd discovery is the third HTTP surface, and its scheme is the operator's to choose. Config.TLS is nsqd's TLS, on the protocol connection; what discovery does is decided by the scheme in each LookupdAddrs entry, and go-nsq prepends http:// when there is none. So a scheme-less or http:// lookupd address is cleartext discovery even with Config.TLS set, and that is not refused: nsqlookupd is a different service from nsqd, a TLS nsqd behind a plain-HTTP nsqlookupd on a private network is a real layout, and the traffic carries no credential, though only because this driver turns go-nsq's LookupdAuthorization off: that field defaults to true, and with an AuthSecret configured the discovery poll carried Authorization: Bearer <secret> in the clear. Measured, and the opposite of what was first recorded. The secret now goes to nsqd's AUTH handshake and nowhere else. What it discloses is the topic name in the query and the nsqd host names in the answer. Write https:// and Config.TLS is what the poller uses — its roots, its client certificate and its InsecureSkipVerify. That last part was not true before: go-nsq builds its own lookupd transport with TLSClientConfig unset, so an https:// lookupd verified against the system roots and a private CA failed as an unknown authority. Both halves are measured on the wire.

    Known gotchas¶

    1. A channel must exist before the publication you want it to see. A topic hands its backlog to the first channel that appears; a channel created later starts empty. Use Config.EnsureTopology or Admin.EnsureSubscription.
    2. OK is not durable. nsqd's queue is in memory up to --mem-queue-size, so a publication acknowledged by an nsqd that then dies uncleanly is gone. ConfirmDurable is refused rather than silently weakened.
    3. An empty message cannot be published in a mode that does not envelope: nsqd answers a zero-length body with E_BAD_MESSAGE.
    4. go-nsq's defaults would break the core's retry accounting, and this driver turns two of them off. MaxAttempts defaults to five and what go-nsq does when a message passes it is call FINISH — a message discarded by the client library while the core was still retrying it, which the specification makes a disqualifier. MaxBackoffDuration defaults to two minutes and its backoff sets RDY to zero for the whole subscription. Both are zero here, and TestCriticalGoNSQRetriesAreOff is what keeps them there.
    5. output_buffer_timeout has a floor of 25 ms and a default of 250 ms. go-nsq's default adds a quarter of a second to the latency of every otherwise-idle delivery; this driver's default is nsqd's floor, and nsqd refuses anything below it in the IDENTIFY handshake.
    6. A #ephemeral topic or channel is deleted when its last client goes away, taking whatever it held. The name is legal here and the consequence is nsqd's.
    7. Two nsqd instances are two independent brokers. There is no replication, no partitioning and no cross-instance ordering; a topic exists separately on each nsqd that has seen a publication for it, and a consumer without nsqlookupd sees only the ones it was told about.
    8. Attempts is nsqd's and counts deliveries, including the ones this driver refused to hand out because their lease had gone. A retry budget keyed on it is therefore slightly conservative, which is the safe direction.
    9. Config.LeaseGuard must be shorter than the core's lease-extension lead. It is how early the driver gives a delivery back as well as how much slack a settlement demands, so a mq.WithLeaseExtension(lead) with lead below it means the driver requeues the delivery first and the extension never happens. Extension is opt-in and the default lead is zero, so a default configuration has nothing to trip over.
    10. The conformance run lowers the fidelity figure from 100,000 to 10,000. Every publication here is a PUB answered by nsqd, and the scenario publishes them one at a time; the specification asks a driver that lowers the figure to say so, and this is saying so.

    Driver-specific critical tests¶

    The specification names "NSQ deferred requeue, backoff, lookupd changes" for this driver. They are TestCritical* in drivers/nsq, run by make critical DRIVER=nsq and by make ci, against nsqfake — an in-process model of a channel's message lifecycle, because every one of them is a decision the broker makes and a real nsqd cannot be asked to make any of them on demand.

    Test What it holds
    TestCriticalDeferredRequeue three requeues with rising delays: each comes back only after its own delay, with its attempt count one higher, and never through a client-library backoff
    TestCriticalGoNSQRetriesAreOff twelve negative settlements, past go-nsq's default MaxAttempts twice over, and the broker finishes nothing: a message the client library gave up on would have been finished and never delivered again
    TestCriticalLookupdIsPreferredOverDirectAddresses a configuration with a lookupd discovers through it, and one without connects directly
    TestCriticalARedeliveryAfterAConnectionLossIsNotSettledTwice eight messages redelivered at their timeout, as nsqd does after a connection loss, and again before the consumer's own view of the lease ran out: every settlement from the first round is refused and none of them reaches the broker

    The model reproduces nsqd's exact laxity — a FIN, REQ or TOUCH names a message identifier and lands on whatever is under it — and counts every settlement that landed on the wrong delivery attempt. A model that refused a stale settlement would make the driver's defence untestable, because the test would pass with the defence removed.

    Beside them, TestCloseRacesTheHandlerForThousandsOfRounds is this repository's recurring defect class in the shape it takes here: a delivery placed in the ring while the source is closing. Three thousand rounds, every other delivery deliberately left unsettled, and the conservation law it asserts is that after Close every message the broker took is either finished or back in the channel and none is still in flight.
